ProjectPro: 20 more customers, yet $3.24M of revenue lost

ProjectPro is a B2B project-management platform, three years on the market. New subscriptions keep growing, but so does churn. The task: calculate churn and retention by segment, size the revenue loss, pick the most problematic segment, propose a retention strategy and the metrics that will show it works.

Churn and retention: the key decision is the denominator

The formula is simple: churned divided by the base. But which base? The same business looks either like a disaster or “fine”. Switch the method and watch the numbers move.

Calculation method

The standard is the start-of-period base

The honest answer is a range, not one number

If some of the churned joined this year, churn of the start-of-year base is lower. The brief doesn’t say how many.

How to put it in a meeting: “Churn is between X% and Y%; I’ll be precise after the cohort analysis.” More honest than a single number, and it names the data we need.

Retention by segment

Retention = 1 − churn: share of start-of-year customers who renewed

What this step shows

  • Startups lost more than half of their base (55.1%), not “almost half” — that kind of slip costs credibility in a meeting.
  • The bigger the customer, the better the retention: 44.9% → 68.1% → 82.3%. Typical for B2B: switching tools is expensive for a large company.
  • The worst percentage is not yet the biggest problem. Let’s look at the money first.

Segment payback: LTV to CAC

CAC is not in the brief — move the sliders to see at what acquisition cost a segment stops paying back

LTV = annual plan × customer lifetime; lifetime ≈ 1 / churn

This is a question for marketing: what does one customer cost to acquire in each segment? If a startup costs $3,000, pays $2,400 and stays under two years, the segment barely pays back — so it needs cheaper service, not retention at any cost.

Why they leave: 60% never reached the value

The only clue about causes in the brief is how deeply customers used the product. It needs a careful reading — without passing off a hypothesis as fact.

Churned by depth of use

From the brief (changes with filters, via the demo breakdown)

How to read it

  • Is the product bad, or did the customer never reach the value? 60% used only basic features — most likely the latter: they paid for a tool they never adopted.
  • But 40% used everything and still left. Onboarding solves only part of the problem: these customers had other reasons.
  • Both branches are hypotheses, tested with customer interviews (churned and active) and an exit survey at cancellation.

Strategy: three focuses, not 15 improvements

Strategy answers “what we change”; interviews and the exit survey answer “how we’ll know we’re right”. One focus per segment: what we do and what we deliberately don’t spend on.

Main focus · $1.29M

Mid-market

Get the customer to core value in the first 30 days (adoption)
  • Role-based onboarding: managers see team workload and reports in week one, not just tasks
  • In-product tips and a “3 key features” checklist; an alert to the account manager if an account is stuck on basics
  • An exit survey at cancellation to learn what drove away the 40% who used everything
Not doing: upselling premium features. The goal is for customers to use what they already pay for. Once the product is part of the work, nobody leaves it.
Metric: share of accounts adopting key features within 30 days; time to value
Growth funnel

Startups

Low-cost service and a growth path into the mid-market plan
  • Self-serve: onboarding without a manager, a knowledge base, project templates
  • Catch the growth moment (team > 15, new seats) and move smoothly to the mid-market plan
  • Tag segment moves in the data so growth isn’t counted as churn
Not doing: screening for “promising” startups or expensive sales-led work. Even VCs can’t predict who survives, and a filter would cut inflow.
Metric: share of startups upgraded to mid-market (upgrade rate); CAC payback
Account-level defence · $36,000 per account

Enterprise

Customer Success Managers and early risk warnings
  • A dedicated CSM for every enterprise account: regular check-ins, a success plan
  • An account health score and an alert 90 days before renewal if it drops
  • A quarterly value review with the client’s leadership: what was saved, what’s next
Not doing: broad product changes for 23 accounts. We work with each customer individually.
Metric: health score, share of accounts at risk, segment NRR

Where to start: test the hypotheses

Cheapest knowledge first — days of work, not months of building

  • 1Cohort analysisThe churned by join month and segment. Answers: are old customers leaving, or newcomers who never onboarded?
  • 2Interviews with mid-market8–10 churned and 8–10 active: what was unclear, which jobs went to another tool, which features they didn’t know about.
  • 3Exit surveyOne form at cancellation: price, competitor, missing feature X, team changed, couldn’t figure it out. Without it you can spend six months building the wrong thing.

Why this way

  • Resources go where the money is and where the product can change something.
  • Startups become a funnel into the most valuable segment, not a hole to plug.
  • For expensive customers a person pays off: one CSM per 15–20 accounts at $36,000 protects $0.5–0.7M ARR.

Metrics: churn shows results only a year later

Churn shows up at the next renewal — too late to tell whether the strategy works. We need leading metrics that move within 1–2 months, and lagging ones to report the result.

Strategy metrics

Leading metrics show whether the strategy works; lagging ones show the outcome

How these metrics were chosen

  • A metric is a number you can calculate and compare with last month. “Track” and “ask” are actions, not metrics.
  • Each strategic focus has its own leading metric. If it doesn’t move in 1–2 months, we change tactics without waiting for annual churn.
  • Lagging metrics are counted in accounts and in dollars: the case showed they tell different stories.

Appendix

Method, formulas and assumptions

What comes from the brief, what is calculated and what is simulated for demonstration.

From the brief

Start-of-year, new and churned customers in three segments; average annual plan; the 40/60 feature-usage split among the churned. Every headline figure of the case uses only these.

Calculated

Churn, retention, lost and end ARR, GRR, segment shares, lifetime and LTV, net base change, simulator scenarios.

Demo data

Cards for 2,045 accounts: names, industries, staff, months, seats, health score, exit reasons, the 60/40 split by segment (290 / 74 / 11 “basic only” of 468 / 134 / 23). Totals match the brief; details are illustration, not fact.

Assumptions

In the demo data, 20% of the churned joined this same year. Segment controllability (low / high) is a judgement. CAC is a slider; the brief has none. Price within a segment is uniform — the average plan.

Formulas

Churn = churned in year / customers at start of year
Retention = 1 − churn = renewed / customers at start of year
ARR lost = churned × annual plan
GRR = 1 − ARR lost / start ARR
NRR = (start ARR − losses + upgrades) / start ARR
LTV = plan × 1 / churn; LTV : CAC ≥ 3 — the segment pays back

Traps this analysis avoids

  • Using “start + new” as the denominator — understates churn and hides the problem.
  • Counting only logo churn — with very different plan sizes in B2B, it distorts the picture.
  • Calling the segment with the worst rate the most problematic without looking at money and controllability.
  • Passing off a hypothesis (“never reached value”) as fact and building without interviews.
  • Confusing retention with upsell: the goal is for customers to use what they already pay for.
