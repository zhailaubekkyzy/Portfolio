Product case · Retention & churn

Retention BookLover

BookLover is a mobile reading app launched six months ago. Acquisition grows every month, yet since April 30-day retention has been falling: from 45% to 28%. I found when and where retention breaks, separated causes into “who arrived” and “what they found”, and proposed three hypotheses with metrics and a test plan.

The 30-second version 3 bets

Two problems: growth is buying the wrong users, and the product does not lead readers to a second book

  • What happenedFrom the April cohort on, D30 retention falls and does not recover: 45 → 38 → 30 → 28%. This is a lasting shift, not a one-off incident.
  • WhereRetained users have stalled at about 8,000 a month while new users grew 50%. Almost all growth churns. In May part of the core audience went too.
  • ChronicOnly one user in five reaches a second book. A finished book is the moment of success and also the exit point.
  • What I doA bridge to the second book, cleaning up acquisition channels, a reading habit in week one. Each bet has a metric, a success threshold and a guardrail.
Step 1 · Cohorts

Retention held at 45% for three months, then broke

D30 retention = users active on day 30 after sign-up ÷ users who signed up that month. Total active users kept growing, so the problem is invisible there: growing acquisition masks it. Cohorts separate product quality from marketing power.

“Retained from growth” is the share of a month’s extra users who stayed: Δ retained ÷ Δ new. In January–March every new wave retained like the base (45%). In April only 260 of 4,000 extra users stayed.

Step 2 · Diagnosis

Three conclusions from the shape of the curve

The drop is obvious. Its shape matters more, because it points to the type of cause.

01

The shift is lasting, not an incident

An incident usually gets fixed within days: one cohort dips and the next ones return to 45%. Here May and June did not recover, and June settled at 28%. Something permanent changed: the audience, the product or the market.

02

Growth barely retains

Retained users have been around 8,000 a month since March, while sign-ups grew 50%. Of April’s extra 4,000 people, 6.5% stayed. This is the main signal that marketing scaled into a less relevant audience.

03

In May the core got hurt too

If the problem were only new channels, the May cohort would retain at least as many as March: 8,100 people from the same 18,000 “old” sources. It retained 7,500. So people who would have come anyway also did worse. That points to a product change: a release, the catalog, pricing. The rating drop from 4.5 to 4.2 supports this but does not prove it.

Where the product loses readers

The 65% and 30% figures are a six-month aggregate with no cohort split. So this is a chronic problem, not the cause of the April drop.

Insight. 45% of users finished a book and stopped. They came to read a book, read it, job done. The app is good at helping people read but does not help them choose what to read next. The moment of highest satisfaction becomes the exit point.

About the 4.5 → 4.2 rating. Timing alone does not prove cause. The rating can fall because the product got worse, or because people arrived who were never a fit. Check: rating by app version and by acquisition channel.

Step 4 · Hypotheses

Three bets in “If… then… because…” form

Each hypothesis follows the chain: observation → insight → solution → metric. The “because” holds an assumption about people’s behavior. That is what the test checks.

H1

A bridge to the second book

  1. Observation65% finished, but only 30% of them started a second book: 45% of all users stopped after the first.
  2. InsightChoosing the next book is separate work that the app leaves to the person right when their job is already done.
  3. SolutionA last-page screen: 3 personal picks (same author, genre, “readers of this book chose”) and the first chapter in one tap.
  4. MetricShare of finishers who start a second book within 7 days.

Ifwe show 3 personal picks on the last page and open the first chapter in one tap, thenthe 7-day second-book rate rises from 30% to 40%, and D30 retention by 2–3 pp, becausepeople are willing to keep reading but do not want to spend effort choosing once the job is done.

How I test it. A/B test on new users who reach the end of a book. About 360 finishers per arm detect +10 pp (α = 0.05, 80% power). Runs until every participant has lived 30 days.

What would disprove it. The rate rises but reading time on the second book and D30 do not: people open the pick but do not read. Then the problem is pick quality, not the moment.

H2

Acquire readers, not installs

  1. ObservationSince April retained users sit near 8,000 while acquisition grows. 6.5% of April’s growth retained, vs 45% in January–March.
  2. InsightMarketing optimizes for installs and scales into people who never planned to read. A cheap install becomes an expensive reader.
  3. SolutionMeasure cost per retained reader by channel, cut channels with D30 under 20%, align creatives with what the app actually offers.
  4. MetricD30 retention by channel and cost per retained user (CAC ÷ D30).

Ifwe move budget from channels with D30 under 20% to channels above 40% and drop offers that promise what the product lacks, thenblended D30 retention rises from 28% to 34–38%, and cost per retained reader falls 10–15% on the same budget, becausethe drop began with accelerated acquisition, and almost all the growth churned within 30 days.

How I test it. Not an A/B test but a 4-week budget shift with a control set of channels. Diagnosis first: split April–June cohorts by channel. If every channel retains badly, the hypothesis falls and focus moves to the product. The estimate comes from the dashboard’s budget simulator. The remaining gap closes with the Android v2.0 fix: about +5 pp.

What would disprove it. Organic and the original channels also fell to around 30%. Then the cause is the product, not the audience.

H3

A reading habit in week one

  1. Observation35% of users never finish even their first book.
  2. InsightPeople start a book on impulse, but with no rhythm reading breaks on day 2–3 and the book is abandoned.
  3. SolutionOnboarding: 3 favorite genres, a “10 minutes a day” goal, a reminder at a chosen time and visible book progress.
  4. MetricFirst-book completion, D7 and D30 retention.

Ifat sign-up we offer a 10-minutes-a-day goal with a reminder at a chosen time and a pick based on 3 genres, thenfirst-book completion rises from 65% to 70%, and D7 retention by 3 pp, becausehabits come from repeating at the same time and a small goal, not from day-one motivation.

How I test it. A/B test on new users: about 1,400 per arm for +5 pp completion. Guardrails: notification opt-outs and uninstalls.

What would disprove it. Completion rises but D30 does not. Then the first book is not the main lever, and the bridge to the second (hypothesis 1) matters more.

How many users a test needs

The smaller the expected effect, the larger the sample. Two-sided test, α = 0.05, 80% power:

  • Second book 30% → 40%: ≈ 360 finishers per arm
  • Second book 30% → 36%: ≈ 960 per arm
  • Completion 65% → 70%: ≈ 1,400 per arm
  • D30 retention 28% → 31%: ≈ 3,600 per arm

With 27,000 new users a month any of these tests fills its sample in 1–2 weeks. The constraint is time: D30 can only be measured 30 days after the last participant joins.

Plan

Cheap knowledge first, expensive builds later

  1. Week 1 · DiagnosisSQL: cohorts by channel, campaign, platform and app version. In parallel, 6–8 interviews with people who finished and stopped and with April churners. Review 1–3★ ratings since April.
  2. Week 2 · Channel decisionIf channels are to blame: shift budget (hypothesis 2). If the product: find the April change and roll it back or fix it. Design the last-page screen.
  3. Weeks 3–4 · Launch testsA/B test of the second-book bridge (hypothesis 1). Prepare the habit onboarding (hypothesis 3).
  4. Weeks 5–9 · DecisionsRead D7 and D30, check guardrails, decide: scale, iterate or kill. Write down what we learned about users even if the test lost.

SQL: retention by cohort and channel

The first query of week 1. Tables users (id, created_at, channel) and events (user_id, event_time).

-- D30 retention by monthly cohort and channel
WITH cohort AS (
  SELECT id AS user_id,
         DATE_TRUNC('month', created_at) AS cohort_month,
         channel,
         created_at
  FROM users
),
active_d30 AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT c.user_id
  FROM cohort c
  JOIN events e ON e.user_id = c.user_id
  WHERE e.event_time >= c.created_at + INTERVAL '28 days'
    AND e.event_time <  c.created_at + INTERVAL '31 days'
)
SELECT c.cohort_month,
       c.channel,
       COUNT(*)                                       AS new_users,
       COUNT(a.user_id)                               AS active_d30,
       ROUND(100.0 * COUNT(a.user_id) / COUNT(*), 1)  AS retention_d30
FROM cohort c
LEFT JOIN active_d30 a ON a.user_id = c.user_id
GROUP BY 1, 2
ORDER BY 1, retention_d30;

How a PM thinks

The method behind this case

Eight steps that repeat in any retention problem. Order matters: each step narrows the field for the next one.

  1. Frame the problemWhich metric, by how much, since when, for whom. “Retention is falling” is not a problem statement. “D30 of new users fell from 45% to 28% starting with the April cohort” is.
  2. Sanity-check the dataHow is “active” defined? Has the last cohort completed its 30 days? Did tracking change? Don’t confuse an aggregate with a split: 65% and 30% here are totals, not per cohort.
  3. Size itTurn percentages into people and money: ≈ 9,900 active users lost in 3 months. Scale decides how much effort the problem deserves.
  4. DecomposeMix or core? Retention = who arrived × what they found. Compare old vs new channels, app versions, funnel stages.
  5. Observation → insight → solution → metricA fact from the data, an explanation through human behavior, a product change, a number to check it. An insight is not a restated number but an answer to “why”.
  6. Write the hypothesisIf [action], then [metric] moves by [amount], because [behavior]. Plus what would disprove it. Without a number and a threshold it cannot be tested.
  7. PrioritizeICE or RICE. Start with what is cheap and teaches the most: SQL and interviews cost days, builds cost weeks.
  8. Design the test and decidePrimary metric, guardrails, sample size, duration, stopping rule — before launch. After — scale, iterate or kill, and record what was learned.

Traps I avoid

  • Looking at total actives instead of cohorts
  • Filling data gaps to fit the hypothesis: “it must have dropped”
  • Confusing timing with cause (rating and retention)
  • Scaling power-user behavior without a test: they might read anywhere
  • Picking a tool before the problem: RFM without revenue data
  • A hypothesis with no number: “retention will go up”

Where the method comes from

The Build–Measure–Learn loop and testing assumptions come from Eric Ries’s The Lean Startup. The hypothesis template “We believe… will result in… we’ll know when…” comes from Jeff Gothelf’s Lean UX. The four-link chain is a simplified scheme I use for practice.
